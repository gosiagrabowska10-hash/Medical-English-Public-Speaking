### Introduction

Most medical presentations fail for a reason that has nothing to do with English. The speaker knows too much, shows too much and never says plainly what the audience should take away. After a busy session, colleagues remember perhaps one idea from each talk — and if the speaker has not chosen that idea, the audience chooses it for them, often from a detail on slide seven. This lesson gives you a disciplined way to decide what your talk is for before you open your slide software. You will learn to reduce a body of work to one core message, to test that message against the audience in front of you, and to build two or three supporting points that carry it. The method is the foundation for the rest of this module: openings and closings in L11 and slide design in L12 both depend on a message you can state in one sentence. It also works in a second language, because a clear message protects you when words are hard to find: you always know where you are going.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write a one-sentence core message for a presentation that states a claim, not a topic
- Test a core message against the audience, the setting and the time available, and revise it
- Select two or three supporting points and the evidence for each, and cut material that does not serve the message
- Deliver a 30-second spoken version of your message without slides

### Teaching content

#### Why experts struggle to simplify

Doctors who present are usually the people who know the subject best in the room. That is exactly the difficulty. Once you know something well, it becomes hard to imagine not knowing it: abbreviations feel universal, the reasoning feels obvious, and every result feels important because you remember the effort behind it. The audience has none of this context. They meet your work for the first time, at speaking speed, with no way to scroll back.

The practical consequence is that experts overestimate what an audience can absorb and underestimate how much guidance it needs. A clear core message is the simplest correction. It forces you to decide what matters from the audience’s point of view, not from the point of view of the person who did the work.

> **Research evidence.** In economic experiments, better-informed participants were unable to ignore their own knowledge when predicting the judgements of less-informed people — an effect the authors called the “curse of knowledge”. The studies did not concern presentations, but they describe the same bias that makes expert talks hard to follow. [1]

#### Topic, title and message are not the same

A topic is what you talk about: “delirium screening in older surgical patients”. A title is how the programme lists it. A message is what you want the audience to believe or do when you have finished: “Screening every older surgical patient on the first post-operative day detects delirium a day earlier, and it takes two minutes per patient.” Only the message gives the talk a direction.

A useful test is grammatical. A topic is a noun phrase. A message is a complete sentence with a verb, and usually a claim that someone could disagree with. If your sentence cannot be challenged — “Delirium is an important problem” — it is still a topic. If it includes too many clauses to say in one breath, it is still a summary of everything you did, not a message.

Good medical messages are specific about the population, the finding and the consequence. They also respect the strength of the evidence. A single-centre observational study supports “was associated with”, not “prevents”. Precision here is not only a matter of style; it protects your credibility with expert audiences who will notice an overclaim at once.

#### Working memory sets the limits

Listeners hold only a small amount of new information in mind at once. When a talk asks them to track six findings, four abbreviations and a complex graph at the same time, they do not simply remember less of everything; they lose the thread that connects the pieces. Written text can be reread. Speech cannot.

This is why the Academy recommends one core message and no more than three supporting points for a short talk. Each supporting point should be a sentence in its own right, and each should clearly support the message. Additional findings can go into a backup slide, a handout or the answer to a question. Leaving them out of the talk is not hiding them; it is choosing what the audience can actually use.

> **Research evidence.** A widely cited review of research on short-term memory concluded that adults can hold roughly three to five meaningful units (“chunks”) in mind at once. Presentations are not memory experiments, but the limit explains why a small number of clearly grouped points is easier to follow than a long list. [2]

#### Building the message: four questions

Write your message before you open your slides, by answering four questions in order. First: who is in the room, and what do they already know? A message for cardiologists at a specialist congress differs from one for a hospital management board. Second: what do they care about? Patients, workload, cost, safety, training or an open scientific question. Third: what is the single most important thing your work shows them? Fourth: what should they think or do differently afterwards?

Then combine the answers into one sentence and say it aloud. Many speakers find a simple narrative frame helpful: what is known, what is the problem or gap, and therefore what you found or propose. For example: “We know that delirium is common after surgery, but it is often detected late on busy wards; therefore we tested a two-minute screen on day one, and it found delirium a day earlier.” Shortened to its core, this becomes the message.

Finally, check the message against the time available. A five-minute oral presentation can carry one message and one or two points. A forty-minute grand round can carry the same message with three well-developed points and a discussion of limitations. The message stays constant; the depth changes.

> **Professional recommendation.** The “And, But, Therefore” frame and the problem–gap–finding sequence are widely taught in science communication. They are practical tools, not research findings: use them to draft, then revise the sentence in your own words. [3, 4]

#### Supporting points and the evidence for each

Once the message is fixed, every part of the talk must earn its place by supporting it. List everything you could include — results, methods, background, anecdotes — and sort each item into one of three groups: essential to support the message, useful for questions, and interesting to you but not to this audience. Only the first group goes into the talk.

Arrange the supporting points in the order that makes the message most convincing. For research, this is often problem, method in brief, key result and implication. For a clinical guideline or service change, it may be the current problem, the proposal and what it will mean for the team. Each point should end by connecting back to the message, so the audience hears the same idea from several angles.

Clinicians already use structured formats when communication must be fast and safe. One example is SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation), a handover and escalation tool used in many hospitals. It exists because a clear structure reduces the effort needed to understand a message under pressure. SBAR is not a presentation framework, and it is mentioned here only as an analogy: a presentation is a slower setting with different aims, but the underlying principle — say what matters in a predictable order — is the same. Familiarity with SBAR varies between countries and institutions; if it is not used where you work, the same idea applies to any structured handover format you know.

> **Research evidence.** SBAR was adopted in clinical settings as a shared structure for communication between clinicians; reports of its implementation describe improved clarity and a common model for escalating concerns. It is cited here as an example of structured clinical communication, not as evidence about presentations. [5]

#### The thirty-second test

Before you build a single slide, explain your talk aloud in thirty seconds to someone outside your specialty, or record yourself if no one is available. Say the message, name the supporting points and stop. If you cannot do it, the talk is not yet ready, however polished the slides are.

The test has a second benefit for speakers working in a second language. A message you have said aloud many times becomes a phrase you can produce under pressure. When you lose a word in the middle of a talk, you can always return to it: “The point I want you to take away is this: …” Your message becomes both the destination of the talk and a safe place to come back to.

### Professional examples

*Illustrative, composite examples; names and details are fictional.*

**From a topic to a message** — *Regional anaesthesia meeting, ten-minute oral presentation*

A fictional anaesthetist’s first draft is titled “Experience with ultrasound-guided nerve blocks in our unit” and contains eighteen slides covering technique, equipment, eight outcome measures and a literature review. Asked what the audience should remember, she says: “That we do a lot of blocks.” After the four questions, she writes: “In our unit, adding a block nurse to the team allowed us to provide nerve blocks for hip-fracture patients within four hours of admission, and patients needed less opioid on the first day.” She keeps three outcome measures, moves the rest to backup slides and reduces the talk to nine slides. The talk is shorter, and colleagues ask about staffing — the point she actually wanted to make.

**One message, two audiences** — *Paediatric respiratory service, congress talk and management meeting*

A fictional paediatric team has introduced a nurse-led asthma clinic. At a specialist congress, the message is: “A nurse-led review within two weeks of an emergency attendance was associated with fewer repeat attendances over six months in our population.” At the hospital management meeting the next week, the same work becomes: “A nurse-led review after an emergency attendance was associated with fewer repeat visits in our pilot; extending it to all sites would need one additional nurse post, and its effect should be evaluated as it is extended.” The data are the same. The audience, and therefore the message, are different.

**Saving a journal club** — *Internal medicine journal club, trainee presenting a trial*

A fictional trainee prepares twenty-five slides summarising every table of a randomised trial. A consultant suggests a different approach: “Tell us whether this trial should change what we do on Monday.” The trainee rewrites the talk around one sentence: “This trial supports earlier switching from intravenous to oral antibiotics in stable patients, but its population excludes most of our frail elderly patients.” Two supporting points follow — the result and the limitation — and the discussion that follows is the best of the year, because the audience knows what is being argued.

### Phrase bank

| Expression | Use | Register | Pronunciation | Example in a presentation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The one thing I’d like you to remember is…** | Stating your core message explicitly | Neutral | Stress “ONE”; pause after “is”. | *The one thing I’d like you to remember is this: // two minutes of screening finds delirium a day earlier.* |
| **In short, …** | Giving the message in its shortest form | Neutral – formal | — | *In short, the block nurse made early analgesia possible.* |
| **This matters because…** | Linking a point to the audience’s concerns | Neutral | — | *This matters because late detection means a longer stay.* |
| **I’ll make three points.** | Announcing the number of supporting points | Neutral | Stress “THREE”. | *To support that, I’ll make three points: the problem, the change we made, and the result.* |
| **Which brings me back to my main point:** | Returning to the message after a supporting point | Neutral | — | *Which brings me back to my main point: early screening is feasible on a busy ward.* |
| **What this means for your practice is…** | Turning a finding into a consequence for the audience | Neutral – formal | — | *What this means for your practice is that one question on day one can change management.* |
| **The evidence for this comes from…** | Introducing support for a claim | Formal | — | *The evidence for this comes from our audit of four hundred patients.* |
| **We found an association, not proof of cause.** | Stating the strength of evidence honestly | Formal | — | *We found an association, not proof of cause, but the size of the difference is worth testing.* |
| **I won’t go into the details of…, but…** | Signalling that you are leaving material out | Neutral | — | *I won’t go into the details of the statistics, but they are in the backup slides.* |
| **If you remember nothing else, …** | Repeating the message near the end | Neutral, informal | — | *If you remember nothing else, remember the two-minute screen.* |

*Using these in your presentations:* Write your core message as one sentence. Then say it three times aloud, each time introduced by a different expression from the phrase bank, and record the version that sounds most natural to you. Use that version in your opening, after your last supporting point, and in your close.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Using the title as the message | Write a complete sentence with a verb and a claim someone could disagree with. |
| Including every result because it took work to obtain | Keep only results that support the message; move the rest to backup slides. |
| Overstating the evidence to make the message stronger | Match the verb to the design: “was associated with”, “suggests”, “showed”. |
| Writing the message after the slides | Write and say the message first; build slides to support it. |
| Using the same message for every audience | Keep the data; adapt the consequence to what this audience cares about. |
| Stating the message only once | State it in the opening, return to it after each point and repeat it in the close. |

### Practical exercise — From topic to core message

Turn the topic of one of your own talks into a core message and a short structure.

1. Choose a presentation you will give, or recently gave, and write its title or topic.
2. Answer the four questions in one or two lines each: audience, what they care about, the most important finding, what they should think or do afterwards.
3. Combine the answers into one sentence of no more than 35 words, with a verb and a claim. Check that the verb matches the strength of your evidence.
4. List up to three supporting points, each as a full sentence, and one item you will now leave out.
5. Say the message and the points aloud in 30 seconds, and note what was hard to say.

*Student prompt:* Paste your topic, your answers to the four questions, your one-sentence message, your supporting points, what you left out, and a note on the 30-second test. · *Minimum:* 300 characters

**Self-check**

- The message is a full sentence with a verb and a claim, not a topic.
- The verb matches the evidence (association, feasibility or effect).
- There are no more than three supporting points, each a full sentence.
- At least one item has been deliberately left out.
- You said it aloud within about 30 seconds.

**Model answer** *(fictional example; shown to the student only after they save their own response)*

> Topic: “Our experience with a frailty screen in the emergency department.”
> Audience: emergency physicians and geriatricians at a regional meeting; they know frailty matters but doubt that screening is feasible in a crowded department. They care about: time, flow and avoidable admissions. Most important finding: triage nurses completed the screen in most eligible patients, and screen-positive patients were referred to geriatrics earlier. Afterwards they should: consider piloting the screen at triage.
> Message (35 words): “A one-minute frailty screen at triage was completed for most patients over seventy-five in our department, and geriatric review happened earlier without delaying flow; it may be feasible in similar settings, subject to local evaluation.”
> Supporting points: (1) Frail older patients waited too long for geriatric review. (2) Triage nurses completed the screen in one minute, with brief training. (3) Geriatric review happened earlier, and time in the department did not increase.
> Left out: the comparison of three frailty tools — moved to a backup slide.
> 30-second test: hard to say “screen-positive” naturally; I will say “patients who screened positive”.

### Introduction

The first and last minutes of a presentation do more work than any other part. In the opening, the audience decides whether your talk is relevant to them and whether they can follow you. In the close, they decide what to take away. Many medical talks waste both: they open with thanks, an outline slide and a long background, and they end with “That’s all — any questions?”. In L01 you practised opening without apology, and in L02 you rehearsed your first thirty seconds to manage nerves. This lesson is about content: what an opening and a closing must achieve, which devices work for medical audiences, and how to write both so that they carry the core message you defined in L10. You will finish with a scripted opening and closing for a real talk, and a habit you can use for every presentation, from a five-minute oral to a keynote.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write an opening that establishes relevance, a gap or question, your core message and the route of the talk within about one minute
- Choose an opening device suited to the audience and setting, and avoid devices that waste the first minute
- Write a closing that restates the message, gives a specific take-home and call to action, and ends on a planned final sentence
- Signpost the structure of a talk so that listeners always know where they are

### Teaching content

#### Why beginnings and endings carry so much weight

Many speakers work on the assumption that the start and the end of a talk matter most. The research usually cited for this concerns recall of items in word lists, not attention or memory during presentations (see the evidence note below), so it supports the assumption only by analogy. As a professional recommendation, the conclusion is nevertheless sound: plan a strong opening and a strong closing, put your message in both, and do not spend those moments on administration.

A talk that closes clearly, on its main point, also leaves a different impression from one that runs out of time and stops in the middle of a slide. This is professional experience rather than a research finding. You cannot control everything an audience remembers, but you can make sure that the last thing they hear is the thing you most want them to keep.

Optional note: in a well-known colonoscopy study (reference 2), patients’ later ratings of discomfort depended strongly on the worst moment and the final minutes. It concerns evaluations of medical procedures, not audience memory of presentations, and is not used as evidence in this lesson.

> **Research evidence.** In classic memory research, items at the beginning and end of a list were recalled better than those in the middle (primacy and recency effects). The research used word lists, not presentations; it is cited because it illustrates why the start and the end of a talk deserve special care. [1]

#### The four jobs of an opening

An effective opening does four jobs, usually in this order. It establishes relevance: why this topic matters to these people, today. It opens a gap: the question, problem or uncertainty that your work addresses. It states the message, or at least promises it clearly. And it gives a route: two or three signposts that show how the talk will proceed.

In practice this can take less than a minute. “Every one of us has seen an older patient become confused after surgery. On a busy ward, we often notice it a day or two late. Today I want to show you that a two-minute screen on the first post-operative day finds delirium a day earlier. I’ll show you why we tried it, how it works on the ward, and what happened.” Relevance, gap, message, route — and the audience knows exactly why to listen.

Thanks to the chair and organisers belong in a single short sentence, or in your first answer to a question. Your name and affiliation are usually announced by the chair and printed on the programme. An outline slide that lists “Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion” adds nothing; a spoken route that names your actual points adds a great deal.

#### Opening devices for medical audiences

Four devices work reliably with medical audiences. A short clinical vignette makes the problem concrete: “A seventy-eight-year-old man is admitted with a hip fracture on a Friday night…” It must be fictional or composite and contain no identifiable details: calling a case composite does not make a rare diagnosis, an exact date or place, or a recognisable combination of details acceptable. A striking, verified number gives scale — “one in five patients over seventy” is an illustrative figure only, to be replaced in a real talk by a number you have checked and can cite. A genuine question engages the audience’s curiosity, provided you answer it. A contrast between what people assume and what is true can be powerful, if it does not caricature colleagues.

Some devices rarely work, especially across cultures and languages. Jokes depend on shared references and timing, and they are the first thing to fail in a second language. Famous quotations are often misattributed and feel generic. Rhetorical questions that you do not answer frustrate an expert audience. Dictionary definitions waste time on what everyone knows. And opening with the limitations of your study undermines your message before you have stated it.

> **Research evidence.** Research on curiosity suggests that it arises when people become aware of a gap in their knowledge that they want to close. A well-chosen question or unexpected finding in the opening creates such a gap — provided the talk then closes it. [3]

#### Signposting: keeping the audience with you

Readers can see headings, page numbers and how much text remains. Listeners cannot. Signposts are the spoken equivalent: short phrases that tell the audience where they are, what has just finished and what comes next. “So that was the problem. Now let me show you what we changed.” They feel repetitive to the speaker; to the audience they are a map.

Use the route from your opening as your structure, and refer back to it. If you promised three points, say “The second point is…” and “Finally, the third point…”. At each transition, link the point you have just made to your message. In a second language, signposts have a further advantage: they are fixed phrases that you can prepare in advance, so they give you a moment of fluency and a breath at exactly the points where you change direction.

> **Professional recommendation.** Plan a signpost for every transition between your supporting points and write it on your speaker notes. This is Academy guidance drawn from professional practice in presentation teaching.

#### Closings that are remembered

A strong closing has three parts. First, a signal that you are concluding: “To conclude…” or “Let me finish with…”. Audiences re-engage at this signal, so make sure what follows is worth it. Second, the message, restated in the same words you used in the opening, with the evidence from your supporting points briefly recalled. Third, a take-home and, where appropriate, a call to action: what the audience should think, check or do differently.

A call to action works best when it is specific and realistic. “We should all improve delirium care” changes nothing. “If your ward does not screen on day one, try the two-minute test for one month and audit what you find” gives listeners something they can actually do. Specific plans that say when, where and how are more likely to be acted on than general intentions.

How direct to be depends on the audience, the culture and your position. A trainee addressing senior colleagues, or a guest at another institution, may prefer a diplomatic form: “Would it be worth trying the screen on one ward for a month?” or “I’d like to suggest that we review fluid charts at the morning round.” A softer request should still be specific.

Plan your final sentence word for word and end on it. Then thank the audience, and stop. Avoid ending with “That’s all”, with an apology for running over, or with a slide titled “Questions?” that replaces your message. If there is time for questions, many experienced speakers add a brief return to the message after the last answer: “Thank you for the questions. The point I’d like you to take away is still…”

> **Research evidence.** Research on implementation intentions has found that people are more likely to act on a goal when they specify when, where and how they will act. The studies concern individual goals rather than audiences, but they support making a call to action concrete. [4]

#### Scripting and rehearsing the edges

Most experienced presenters do not script whole talks, but many script the first and last minute word for word. These are the moments when nerves are highest and when the exact words matter most. Write both, read them aloud, and cut every sentence that does not do one of the jobs described above.

Then rehearse them until you can deliver them while looking at the audience rather than at your notes, combining the content from this lesson with the delivery skills from L02, L07 and L08: a pause before the message, a clear falling tone at the end of it, and eye contact on the final sentence. Your opening and closing will become the most reliable parts of every talk you give.

### Introduction

In medicine, slides often become the presentation. They are written as documents, read aloud by the speaker and photographed by an audience that has stopped listening. For a speaker working in a second language the temptation is even stronger: a full slide feels like a safety net. This lesson argues the opposite. Slides work best when they support what you say — showing the evidence for a claim that you make aloud — and when they are designed for people who see each one for a few seconds from the back of a room. You will learn the assertion–evidence approach, how to present data that can be read at a glance, how to make slides accessible to every member of an international audience, and how to speak from your slides without reading them. The lesson builds directly on your core message (L10) and your signposts (L11): every slide should make one point that supports them.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Rewrite a text-heavy slide as an assertion–evidence slide with a sentence headline and visual evidence
- Choose and simplify a chart or table so that its main comparison can be read in a few seconds
- Check a slide for readability and accessibility: text size, contrast, colour use, abbreviations and confidentiality
- Present a slide by speaking from it rather than reading it, using the sequence look, pause, conclude

### Teaching content

#### What slides are for

Audiences can look at a slide and listen at the same time — that is what a good diagram with a clear explanation achieves. The difficulty arises with dense text. When a slide is full of sentences, many listeners start reading it, usually faster or slower than you speak, and they follow your voice less closely. The most specific problem is redundancy: if you then read the same sentences aloud, identical on-screen text and narration compete for the same attention instead of complementing each other. For many listeners the result is a talk that is harder to follow than either the slide or the speech alone.

Slides are most useful for what speech cannot do well: showing images, data, anatomy, processes and relationships. Text on slides should be limited to what helps the audience to see the point — a headline, labels and a few words of emphasis. Detailed methods, references and the full results belong in a handout, a backup slide or the published paper.

> **Research evidence.** In studies of multimedia learning, people learned less when identical on-screen text was presented at the same time as narration than when they heard the narration alone — the “redundancy effect”. A psychological analysis of slide presentations also found frequent violations of principles of perception, memory and comprehension. These studies were not of medical congress talks, but they consistently point in the same direction. [1, 2, 3]

#### One slide, one point: the assertion–evidence approach

The assertion–evidence approach replaces the familiar topic heading and bullet list with two elements. The headline is a complete sentence — an assertion — that states the point of the slide: “Readmissions fell by a third after the nurse-led review”, not “Results”. The body is visual evidence for that assertion: a graph, an image, a diagram or a small table, with minimal text.

This format has three advantages. The audience knows the point even if they look away for a moment. You know what to say, because the headline is your spoken sentence. And the sequence of headlines, read on their own, becomes the argument of the talk — a useful check that every slide supports the core message from L10. If two consecutive headlines say the same thing, merge the slides; if a headline does not connect to your message, the slide probably belongs in the backup section.

> **Research evidence.** In studies with engineering students, sentence headlines produced better recall of the main points than phrase headlines, and slides designed with an assertion and visual evidence produced better comprehension than topic-and-bullet slides. The studies were conducted in educational settings, not at medical congresses, so treat the size of the effect with caution. [4, 5]

#### Data that can be read in seconds

Most medical slides contain data, and most data slides contain too much. Begin by deciding which single comparison supports the headline. Then choose a format that makes that comparison easy to see. People judge positions along a common scale, such as the heights of bars from the same baseline or points on the same axis, more accurately than angles, areas or colour intensity. This is why a simple bar or dot chart is usually clearer than a pie chart or a three-dimensional graph.

Simplify ruthlessly. Remove gridlines, unnecessary decimal places, legends that can be replaced by direct labels, and every series that does not serve the point. Highlight the comparison that matters — for example, the intervention group in a strong colour and the control in grey — and label it directly. If you must show a table, reduce it to the rows and columns the audience needs, and highlight the key cell.

When you present the slide, apply the sequence from L07: tell the audience where to look, pause while they look, and then state the conclusion in the words of your headline. A data slide without this guidance asks every member of the audience to interpret the graph alone, in a few seconds, and they will not all reach the same conclusion.

> **Research evidence.** Experiments on graphical perception found that judgements of position along a common scale were more accurate than judgements of length, angle or area. This supports bar and dot charts over pie charts when exact comparisons matter. [6]

#### Readable and accessible slides

Design for the person in the back row, on a small screen in a hybrid session, and with less-than-perfect vision. As a recommended minimum for a standard 16:9 slide — professional guidance, not a universal rule — use body text of at least 24 points and headlines of at least 32 points, a plain sans-serif font, and strong contrast between text and background. Where a congress provides a template or its own size rules, follow them, and let the actual room, screen size and viewing conditions take precedence: a large hall may need larger text. Test each slide by viewing it at the size of a laptop screen from two metres away, or by shrinking it to a quarter of the screen: if you cannot read it, neither can your audience.

Do not rely on colour alone to carry meaning. Red–green colour vision deficiency is common, especially among men, so a graph in which the only difference between groups is red versus green may be unreadable for several people in a large audience. Use colour together with labels, line styles or position, and choose colour pairs such as blue and orange that remain distinguishable.

For international audiences, write abbreviations in full at first use, avoid local acronyms and idioms, and keep the language of slides simple. Never show identifiable patient information, including faces, names, dates of birth or rare combinations of details, without explicit consent and approval under your institution’s policies. Use images and figures only when you have the right to do so, and acknowledge their sources.

> **Research evidence.** A review of worldwide data reported that red–green colour vision deficiency affects about 8% of men and about 0.4% of women of northern European origin, with lower but still important prevalence in other populations. [7]

#### Speaking from slides, not reading them

If your slides contain only headlines and evidence, you cannot read them aloud — which is the point. You need a different safety net: speaker notes with a few keywords for each slide, the headline sentence you will say, the number you must protect, and the signpost that leads to the next slide. Practise each slide by looking at it for two seconds, turning to the audience and saying the point in your own words.

Plan the moment each slide appears. Let the audience look at a new slide for a second or two before you speak, or tell them what to look at first. Use the blank or black slide function when you want attention back on you, for example during a story or a key message. And prepare backup slides after your closing slide for details you expect to be asked about; showing one in answer to a question is far more effective than squeezing it into the talk.

Finally, check the practical details early: the congress template and aspect ratio, embedded fonts, videos that play without an internet connection, and a copy of the file on a memory stick and in your email. Many apparent slide problems on the day are file problems that could have been prevented.

> **Professional recommendation.** Before every talk, read only your slide headlines in order. If they tell the story of your talk and support your core message, the structure is sound. This is an Academy practice recommendation.

**1. What is an assertion–evidence slide?**

- A. A slide with a topic heading and five bullet points
- B. A slide with a sentence headline stating the point and visual evidence supporting it **✓ correct**
- C. A slide that contains only a quotation
- D. A slide that lists all the references

*Explanation shown to the student:* The sentence headline states the point; the body shows the evidence, with minimal text.

**2. [Scenario] Your results slide has nine lines of text, which you read aloud. Why is this likely to reduce comprehension?**

- A. Because reading aloud is impolite
- B. Because congress rules forbid text
- C. Because the audience reads and listens at the same time, and identical text and speech compete for attention **✓ correct**
- D. Because nine is an unlucky number

*Explanation shown to the student:* Research on the redundancy effect found that identical on-screen text and narration presented together can reduce learning.

**3. [Scenario] You need to compare the proportion of patients with complications in four hospitals. Which format is usually clearest?**

- A. Four pie charts
- B. A three-dimensional cone chart
- C. A table with all values to three decimal places
- D. A bar or dot chart on a common scale, with the key hospital highlighted **✓ correct**

*Explanation shown to the student:* Positions on a common scale are judged more accurately than angles or areas.

**4. Your graph shows the intervention group in red and the control group in green, with no labels. What is the main problem?**

- A. Viewers with red–green colour vision deficiency may not distinguish the groups **✓ correct**
- B. Red is too emotional for medical slides
- C. Green is reserved for positive results
- D. There is no problem if the legend is large

*Explanation shown to the student:* Red–green colour vision deficiency is common. Use colour-blind-safe pairs and add labels, line styles or position.

**5. [Scenario] You want to show a striking clinical photograph of a patient’s face. What must you do first?**

- A. Crop out the eyes
- B. Nothing, if the audience is medical
- C. Lower the image resolution
- D. Obtain explicit consent and follow your institution’s policy, or use an anonymised alternative **✓ correct**

*Explanation shown to the student:* Identifiable images require explicit consent and institutional approval; otherwise use anonymised material.

**6. [Scenario] A new data slide appears. What should you do in the first seconds?**

- A. Start explaining the methods immediately
- B. Tell the audience where to look, pause while they look, then state the conclusion **✓ correct**
- C. Read the axis labels aloud
- D. Move quickly to the next slide

*Explanation shown to the student:* Look, pause, conclude (L07) guides the audience to the right conclusion before they interpret the graph alone.
