### Introduction

Your voice carries your expertise to the back of the room. If listeners cannot hear the end of your sentences, or if every sentence sounds the same, even excellent content becomes hard to follow and easy to forget. Many second-language speakers, concentrating on words and grammar, let their voice fade, flatten or rise in ways they do not intend. This lesson is about using your voice deliberately: projecting it without strain, using intonation to signal certainty, contrast and continuation, and varying pitch, volume and pace so that the audience can hear what matters. Your accent is not the target of this lesson. The aim is a voice that is easy to hear, easy to follow and recognisably yours.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Project your voice to the back of a room, or into a microphone, without strain
- Use falling intonation to make statements and conclusions sound certain, and rising tones deliberately for questions and unfinished lists
- Vary pitch, volume and pace to highlight key information and avoid a monotone delivery
- Use a two-minute voice warm-up and basic voice-care habits on long speaking days

### Teaching content

#### Why the voice matters for comprehension and authority

Audiences use the voice for two things at once. They use it to decode the words, which requires that the words are audible and clearly separated. They also use it to interpret meaning: which information is new, which is important, whether the speaker is certain or tentative, and whether a sentence has finished. In English much of this second layer is carried by intonation and stress rather than by vocabulary.

Speaking in a second language makes both layers vulnerable. Attention spent on formulation is attention not spent on breath and volume, so the voice often fades towards the end of a sentence — exactly where English tends to place new information. The intonation patterns of your first language may also carry over into English, sometimes producing a flatter melody or rises where an English-speaking listener expects a fall. None of this is a matter of accent. A speaker with a strong accent and a well-used voice is easier to follow than a speaker with a mild accent and a monotone.

> **Research evidence.** In a study of oral presentations given in English by second-language speakers, the amount of pitch variation measured in the speakers’ voices was associated with how lively listeners judged the presentations to be. [1]

#### Breath support and projection

Projection is not shouting. A projected voice is supported by breath from the lower ribs and abdomen and directed towards the furthest listener; a shouted voice is pushed from the throat, sounds strained and tires quickly. Stand with your weight even, your shoulders relaxed and your head level. Breathe in low and quietly, so that your lower ribs widen, and let the sound ride on a steady outflow of air.

Aim your voice at the back wall, not at the first row or at your notes. The easiest check is to imagine a colleague sitting in the last row who needs to hear the final word of each sentence. Plan your breathing around your thought groups from Lesson 7: take a breath at every pause, so that you never start an important sentence with an empty chest. A sentence that runs out of air drops in volume at its most important point.

> **Professional recommendation.** Before a talk in an unfamiliar room, ask a colleague to sit in the back row while you say your first two sentences. Adjust until they can hear the last word of each sentence without effort.

#### Working with a microphone

A microphone solves volume, not clarity. It amplifies whatever reaches it, including mumbling and fading sentence endings, and it punishes inconsistency. With a fixed lectern microphone, keep a constant distance of roughly a hand’s width and remember that turning your head to the screen takes your voice out of range. With a lapel microphone, clip it at the centre of the chest, about a hand’s width below the chin, on the side you are less likely to turn away from. With a handheld microphone, hold it below the mouth at a steady angle rather than waving it while you gesture.

The most common problem at congresses is speaking to the slide. If you need to point at the screen, use a simple sequence: turn to the slide and look, then turn back to the audience before you speak. Your voice stays with the microphone and the audience sees your face when they hear the key point.

#### Intonation: falls, rises and what they signal

In standard British English, a falling tone at the end of a statement signals that the statement is complete and that the speaker stands behind it: “Mortality fell by a third.↘” Conclusions, recommendations and key results need a clear fall. A rising tone at the end of a statement can be heard by many listeners as uncertainty or as an invitation to respond. It is not wrong, and in some varieties of English it is common, but in a results presentation it can make firm findings sound tentative.

Rises have important legitimate uses. Yes/no questions often rise: “Did the effect persist?↗” In a list, each item before the last usually rises to show that more is coming, and the last item falls: “We measured mortality,↗ readmissions,↗ and length of stay.↘” A fall–rise on a single word signals a reservation or an unspoken “but”: “The results were encouraging — in the short term.↘↗” Used deliberately, these patterns let you add meaning without adding words.

> **Professional recommendation.** Mark the final word of every key sentence in your script with ↘ and rehearse it with a clear fall. Use ↗ only where you intend a question, an unfinished list or a reservation.

#### Vocal variety: pitch, volume and pace

Monotone delivery usually has a simple cause: reading. When attention is on the written text, pitch, volume and pace settle into a single level. Variety returns when you speak to people about ideas. Use it purposefully. Lower your pitch slightly and slow down for a key message. Increase energy and pace a little for a story or a familiar step. Drop your volume, not just raise it, to draw attention: a quieter sentence after a louder passage makes a room lean in.

Variety should serve meaning, not decoration. Exaggerated, sing-song intonation sounds artificial and can be as distracting as a monotone. The aim is a range that matches the content: a calm, confident baseline, with clear peaks on the words that carry new or important information. Second-language speakers often find that once their script is marked for stress and falls, variety follows naturally.

#### Voice care on long speaking days

Congress days, teaching blocks and on-call shifts place real demands on the voice. Keep water within reach and sip it at section breaks. Avoid repeated throat clearing; a swallow or a sip is gentler. Speak at a comfortable pitch rather than forcing a lower or louder voice than your natural one. Use the microphone when one is offered, even in a mid-sized room.

A short warm-up before speaking prepares the voice in the same way a warm-up prepares any physical skill. It also gives you a structured task in the anxious minutes before a talk, which links it to your routine from Lesson 2. Persistent hoarseness or voice change is a clinical matter that deserves assessment like any other symptom; the techniques in this lesson are educational and do not replace it.

The Academy’s two-minute warm-up has five steps, and none of them needs to be visible to colleagues in a corridor. First, release the shoulders and take three slow, low breaths. Second, hum gently on “mm”, gliding from a low to a comfortable high note and back. Third, do a few lip trills or slides on “ng” to free the voice without strain. Fourth, say two of your own difficult phrases slowly and precisely, such as the title of your talk and a key technical term. Finally, count from one to ten, aiming each number at an imaginary back wall, with a clear fall on “ten”.

> **Research evidence.** A large survey found that voice disorders were reported considerably more often by teachers than by the general population, which illustrates the load that intensive professional speaking places on the voice. [2]

### Professional examples

*Illustrative, composite examples; names and details are fictional.*

**The disappearing last word** — *International cardiology congress, auditorium for 800*

A cardiologist presents a trial with a clear message: “Early treatment reduced hospital admissions by a quarter.” In the back rows, colleagues hear “Early treatment reduced hospital admissions by a…”. His voice falls away on the final words, which carry the result. In rehearsal, a colleague in the last row raises a hand every time a sentence ending is lost. He adds a breath before each key sentence, aims at the back wall and gives “by a QUARTER↘” a clear stress and fall. His content and his accent are unchanged; his result now reaches the whole room.

**Findings that sounded like questions** — *Research meeting, early-career pathologist presenting a validation study*

Each of her result statements ends with a rise: “Sensitivity was ninety-two per cent?↗ Specificity was eighty-eight?↗” Her supervisor notes that the audience began to ask whether she trusted her own data. Rehearsing with falling tones — “Sensitivity was ninety-two per cent.↘ Specificity was eighty-eight.↘” — she keeps rises for her list of next steps and for one genuine question to the audience. The same numbers now sound like findings.

**Turning away from the microphone** — *Grand round, lecture theatre with a fixed lectern microphone*

A respiratory physician explains a CT image while looking at the projection screen. Each time he turns, his voice drops out of the microphone range and the recording of the session has gaps exactly where the findings are described. Using the sequence “turn and look, turn back, then speak”, he points to the image in silence, faces the audience and describes the finding. The pauses also give the audience time to look at the image, as recommended in Lesson 7.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Letting the voice fade at the end of sentences | Breathe at each pause and aim the final word at the back wall. |
| Rising intonation on findings and conclusions | Use a clear fall (↘) on statements you stand behind; keep rises for questions and unfinished lists. |

| Turning to the screen while speaking into a fixed microphone | Turn and look in silence, turn back, then speak. |
| Adding ‘variety’ by exaggerating every sentence | Keep a calm baseline and place peaks only on new or important information. |

### Practical exercise — One sentence, three deliveries

Work on projection and intonation using the key-result sentence of a talk you are preparing.

1. Choose the sentence that carries your main result or recommendation.
2. Mark the stressed word and the final tone (↘ or ↗) on your script.
3. Record the sentence three times: monotone, exaggerated, and purposeful (calm baseline, clear stress, falling end).
4. Place your phone or laptop at the far side of the room and record the purposeful version again with breath support.
5. Listen to all four recordings and note what changed between them.

- The final word of the key sentence has a clear fall in the purposeful version.

### Introduction

Before you say your first sentence, the audience has already formed an impression from how you walk to the lectern, where you look and what your hands are doing. For second-language speakers, physical presence can be a powerful ally: a calm stance, steady eye contact and purposeful gestures signal confidence even when you are searching for a word. They can also work against you, when the effort of speaking English pulls your eyes to the slides and your hands to the clicker. This lesson treats body language as a set of practical, learnable habits rather than a performance. You will work on your stance and the first five seconds, eye contact across a room and through a camera, gestures that support your structure, and the use of space, lectern and screen in person and online.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Establish a stable, open stance and a deliberate start in the first five seconds
- Use eye contact that includes the whole audience, or the camera lens online
- Use purposeful gestures that support structure and meaning, and reduce distracting movement
- Manage space, lectern, slides and clicker in person, online and in hybrid sessions

### Teaching content

#### What audiences read from your body — and what they do not

Audiences form impressions quickly, and nonverbal behaviour contributes to them. A speaker who looks at the audience, stands steadily and moves with purpose is more likely to be perceived as prepared and credible than one who reads from the screen and sways behind the lectern, before either has said anything substantive. This is one reason why the first minute of a talk deserves rehearsal.

Two popular claims deserve caution. The first is that “93 per cent of communication is nonverbal”. This figure is a misreading of experiments from the 1960s that asked listeners to judge a speaker’s feelings from single words spoken in different tones of voice; it does not describe how audiences understand a scientific presentation, where content is essential. The second is that adopting a “power pose” for two minutes changes your hormone levels and makes you more confident. A large replication study did not find the hormonal effects. A stable, open posture is still worth practising, but for simpler reasons: it supports breathing and projection (Lesson 8), it reduces distracting movement, and it is perceived as calm.

Body language is also cultural. Norms for eye contact, distance and gesture vary between countries and between specialties. At international meetings, the most reliable strategy is moderate and readable: steady eye contact without staring, open gestures within the width of your body, and stillness when you deliver key information.

> **Research evidence.** In a well-known study, observers’ ratings of teachers based on very short silent video clips were related to end-of-term student evaluations. The often-quoted “7–38–55” rule comes from experiments on judging attitudes from single words and should not be applied to presentations. A large replication of “power posing” found no effect on hormones. [1, 2, 3]

#### Stance and the first five seconds

A grounded stance is simple: feet roughly hip-width apart, weight evenly distributed, knees soft rather than locked, shoulders relaxed and hands resting at about waist height or loosely by your sides. From this neutral position you can move, gesture and breathe freely. Common habits under pressure — shifting weight from foot to foot, crossing the legs, gripping the lectern or putting hands in pockets — are rarely noticed by the speaker but are very visible to the audience.

The first five seconds follow the routine from Lesson 2: arrive, place your notes, stand in your neutral position, look at the audience, breathe, and only then begin. Starting to speak while still walking, or while looking down at the laptop, makes the opening sound rushed and hides your face at the moment the audience is forming its first impression. The pause will feel long to you and look composed to everyone else.

> **Professional recommendation.** Rehearse your entrance, not just your opening sentence: walk to the lectern, find your stance, look up and breathe before the first word. Two or three rehearsals are usually enough to make it automatic.

#### Eye contact in a room and through a lens

Eye contact tells listeners that you are speaking to them rather than reciting. In a large room, divide the audience mentally into sections — front left, back centre, back right, and so on — and move your gaze between them, holding it with one person for roughly the length of a thought group before moving on. This includes the back and the edges of the room, which speakers tend to forget.

Notes and slides compete for your eyes, especially when you speak in a second language. Use a simple sequence: glance down to collect the next phrase, look up, and then speak. Speaking while reading pulls the voice down into the lectern and the eyes away from the audience; collecting the phrase first and delivering it to a person keeps both. With slides, the same rule applies: look at the screen to check, then turn back before you talk.

Online, the audience experiences eye contact only when you look into the lens. Raise the camera to eye level, place your notes or speaker view just below it, and look into the lens for your opening, your key messages and your conclusion. It is unnatural to look at the lens all the time, and unnecessary; the moments that matter most are the ones to aim for.

#### Gestures that carry meaning

Gestures are not decoration. When they are linked to what you are saying, they help listeners follow you and can help you find your words. Useful presentation gestures are few and easy to learn: counting on your fingers as you enumerate points; showing two sides with two hands when you contrast; a horizontal sweep for a timeline; and an open palm towards the screen when you direct attention to a figure. Keep most gestures within the width of your body and at roughly waist-to-chest height, where they are visible but calm.

Equally important is stillness. Deliver your key message with your hands still and your body facing the audience. Movement just before a key point and stillness during it is a pattern audiences read as emphasis. Distracting movements — clicking the pen, playing with the clicker, adjusting glasses or touching the face — usually increase under stress; noticing your own two or three habits in a recording is the first step to reducing them.

> **Research evidence.** A meta-analysis of studies on gesture and comprehension found that gestures had an overall moderate benefit for listeners’ understanding, with larger effects for gestures depicting physical actions and when gestures were not completely redundant with the words. [4]

#### Space, lectern, slides and clicker

If the room allows it, step away from the lectern for parts of your talk, such as the opening, a story or the conclusion. Move with purpose: walk during transitions between sections and stand still when you make a point. Stand to the side of the screen rather than in front of the projector, ideally on the side that lets you face the audience while gesturing towards the slide.

Use the laser pointer sparingly. A shaking dot draws attention to nervous hands; a slow circle around a feature, held still, does not. Better still, build the emphasis into the slide with a highlight or an arrow, so that you can face the audience. Test the clicker in advance and hold it in your less expressive hand, so that your other hand is free for gestures.

#### Presence online and in hybrid sessions

On a video call, your presence is limited to a small frame, so details matter. Place the camera at eye level, sit or stand so that your head and shoulders fill most of the frame, and position light in front of you rather than behind. Keep gestures inside the frame and a little slower than in a room, so that they are not blurred.

In hybrid sessions you have two audiences. Speak to the room for most of the time, but turn to the camera at defined moments — when you welcome online participants, for key messages and when you take their questions — and say so: “For those joining online…”. This small habit makes remote colleagues feel included and is noticed by chairs and organisers.

### Professional examples

*Illustrative, composite examples; names and details are fictional.*

**Talking to the screen** — *Neurology grand round, case presentation with MRI images*

A specialty trainee stands in front of the screen and describes each image while looking at it, her back half-turned to the audience. Consultants at the back begin checking their phones. In rehearsal she moves to the left of the screen, points with an open hand while silent, turns back and says: “This is the lesion — [pause] — and it is in the brainstem.” Her slides and her words are unchanged. What changed is that the audience now sees her face when she delivers the finding.

**The lectern grip** — *Surgical congress, eight-minute oral presentation*

A colorectal surgeon grips the lectern with both hands for the whole talk and sways slightly forward and back. Watching his recording, he notices that his hands never move, even when he lists three complications. In the next rehearsal he starts with his hands resting on the lectern, then releases them to count “bleeding, leak and infection” on his fingers, and returns to the neutral position for his conclusion. The swaying stops once his weight is even. Colleagues describe the second version as “more confident” without being able to say why.

**An international webinar** — *Online teaching session for physicians in several countries*

An endocrinologist presents from a laptop on her desk. The camera looks up at her from below and she spends most of the session reading her slides on the screen. A colleague suggests three changes: raise the laptop to eye level on a stack of books, move the speaker notes just below the camera, and look into the lens for the opening, each take-home message and the summary. The feedback from participants mentions, for the first time, that she seemed to be “talking to us”.

### Phrase bank

| Expression | Use | Register | Pronunciation | Example in a presentation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Let me step away from the lectern for a moment.** | Signalling a change of position, often before a story or the conclusion | Neutral | — | *Let me step away from the lectern for a moment and tell you about one patient pathway.* |
| **There are three things I’d like you to notice.** | Introducing an enumerating gesture | Neutral | Show the number with your fingers as you say “three”. | *There are three things I’d like you to notice on this slide.* |
| **On the one hand…, on the other hand…** | Presenting a contrast with a two-handed gesture | Neutral – formal | — | *On the one hand, the drug was effective; on the other hand, adherence was poor.* |
| **Let me draw your attention to…** | Directing the audience to part of a slide | Formal | — | *Let me draw your attention to the curve on the right.* |
| **If you look at the top of the slide, …** | Giving a precise visual reference | Neutral | — | *If you look at the top of the slide, you can see the baseline values.* |
| **I’ll come back to the slides in a moment.** | Turning attention from the screen to yourself | Neutral | — | *I’ll come back to the slides in a moment — first, a question for you.* |
| **For those joining online, …** | Including the remote audience in a hybrid session | Neutral | Turn to the camera as you say it. | *For those joining online, please put your questions in the chat.* |
| **Can everyone see the screen clearly?** | Checking visibility in a room or online | Neutral | — | *Before we start, can everyone see the screen clearly?* |
| **Let’s take a step back.** | Moving from detail to the bigger picture, often with a step backwards | Neutral, idiomatic | — | *Let’s take a step back and look at what this means for practice.* |
| **Before I move on, …** | Holding a still moment for a key point before a transition | Neutral | — | *Before I move on, let me repeat the one number to remember.* |

*Using these in your presentations:* Pair each expression with its movement: enumerating fingers for “three things”, two hands for a contrast, a step back for “Let’s take a step back”, a turn to the camera for “For those joining online”. Rehearse three of them standing, as part of your own talk, and record them on video.

### Demonstration (video brief)

The instructor video shows the same two-minute excerpt three times: behind the lectern with no movement; with distracting habits; and with a grounded stance, sectioned eye contact, enumerating and contrasting gestures and a still delivery of the key message. A short online segment demonstrates camera height, framing and looking into the lens.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Starting to speak while still walking or looking at the laptop | Arrive, stand, look up and breathe before the first word. |
| Reading the slides with your back to the audience | Stand beside the screen; look, turn back, then speak. |
| Gripping the lectern or keeping hands in pockets | Use a neutral position at waist height and release your hands for purposeful gestures. |
| Moving constantly or swaying | Move on transitions; stand still for key messages. |
| Looking only at the front row or one friendly face | Move your gaze between sections of the room, including the back and sides. |
| Never looking at the camera online | Look into the lens for the opening, key messages and conclusion. |

### Practical exercise — Video audit of your presence

Record yourself on video delivering a two-minute section of a real talk, then analyse and improve your physical delivery.

1. Record two minutes of a talk standing, as you would deliver it, with the camera showing your whole upper body.
2. Watch the recording once without sound. Note your stance, where you look, what your hands do and any repeated movements.
3. Choose two changes: one to add (for example, enumerating gestures or sectioned eye contact) and one to reduce (for example, swaying or clicker play).
4. Record the same two minutes again with the changes.
5. Compare the two recordings without sound and describe the difference.

*Student prompt:* Describe what you saw in the first recording, the two changes you chose and the difference in the second recording (at least 250 characters). · *Minimum:* 250 characters

**Self-check**

- You watched the first recording without sound.
- You identified at least one habit you had not noticed before.
- The two changes are specific and observable.
- The comparison describes what is visible, not what you intended.

**Model answer** *(fictional example; shown to the student only after they save their own response)*

> First recording (silent viewing): I began speaking while still looking at the laptop. My weight moved from one foot to the other about every ten seconds. I looked at the screen for most of the key-result slide, and I clicked the pen in my left hand throughout.
>
> Changes: add — look up and breathe before the first word, and count the three outcomes on my fingers; reduce — put the pen down and keep my weight even.
>
> Second recording: the opening looks calmer because my face is visible before I speak. The swaying is almost gone. During the key result I looked at the screen once, turned back and said the number to the camera. Without sound, the second version looks like a more senior speaker, although my words were the same.

### Knowledge check (6 questions · pass mark 70%)

**1. What does the often-quoted “93% nonverbal” figure actually come from?**

- A. Studies of scientific presentations
- B. Experiments on judging feelings and attitudes from single spoken words **✓ correct**
- C. A meta-analysis of conference talks
- D. Research on online teaching

*Explanation shown to the student:* The figure comes from experiments on attitudes conveyed by single words and tone of voice. It should not be applied to presentations, where content is essential.

**2. Why does the Academy recommend a stable, open stance?**

- A. Because two minutes of power posing reliably changes hormone levels
- B. Because it supports breathing and projection, reduces distracting movement and is perceived as calm **✓ correct**
- C. Because audiences ignore everything else
- D. Because it hides nervousness completely

*Explanation shown to the student:* The hormonal claims for power posing were not replicated in a large study. The practical benefits of a stable stance remain.

**3. [Scenario] You need to explain an MRI finding in a grand round. Which sequence is best?**

- A. Stand in front of the projector and explain while looking at the image
- B. Stand beside the screen, point in silence, turn back to the audience, then state the finding **✓ correct**
- C. Ask the audience to read the report on the slide
- D. Use the laser pointer continuously while speaking to the screen

*Explanation shown to the student:* Looking, turning back and then speaking keeps your face and voice with the audience at the moment the finding is delivered.

**4. [Scenario] You present online to colleagues in five countries. Where should you look during your key take-home message?**

- A. At the slide on your screen
- B. At your notes
- C. Into the camera lens **✓ correct**
- D. At the participants’ thumbnails

*Explanation shown to the student:* Online, the audience experiences eye contact only when you look into the lens. Prioritise it for the opening, key messages and conclusion.

**5. According to research on gesture and comprehension, when do gestures help listeners most?**

- A. When they are constant and energetic
- B. When they add information that is not completely redundant with the words **✓ correct**
- C. Only when the speaker is a native speaker
- D. Never; gestures distract

*Explanation shown to the student:* A meta-analysis found a moderate overall benefit, larger when gestures depict actions and add information beyond the words.

**6. [Scenario] You are delivering your most important result. What should your body be doing?**

- A. Walking across the stage to add energy
- B. Standing still, facing the audience, with hands calm **✓ correct**
- C. Turning to the slide to read the number
- D. Using the laser pointer in rapid circles

*Explanation shown to the student:* Movement before a key point and stillness during it is a pattern audiences read as emphasis.
