**The practical skill each lesson builds**

- **L10:** turning a topic into a one-sentence core message that makes a claim; four questions (audience, what they care about, the most important finding, what they should think or do); matching the verb to the strength of the evidence (“was associated with” versus “prevents”); no more than three supporting points; deciding what to leave out; the 30-second test. The assignment produces the **message map** that learners bring to Workshop II.
- **L11:** the four jobs of an opening (relevance, gap, message, route); opening devices that work for medical audiences (composite vignette, verified number, genuine question, contrast) and those that rarely do (jokes, quotations, unanswered rhetorical questions, definitions); signposting; closings with a concluding signal, the restated message, a specific call to action and a planned final sentence; returning to the message after questions. It builds on, but does not repeat, L01 (opening without apology) and L02 (the first thirty seconds).
- **L12:** the assertion–evidence slide (sentence headline plus visual evidence); simplifying data so that one comparison can be read in seconds; readable and accessible slides (text size, contrast, colour-blind-safe colours, abbreviations, international audiences); confidentiality and image permissions; speaking from slides without reading them, using “look, pause, conclude” from L07.

**Evidence and accuracy**

- **L10** cites the “curse of knowledge” experiments [Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989] and the review of short-term memory capacity [Cowan, 2001]. Both are presented with an explicit note that they are not studies of presentations. The “And, But, Therefore” frame [Olson, 2015] and the problem–gap–finding sequence [Schimel, 2012] are labelled as **practical tools**, not research findings. SBAR [Haig, Sutton & Whittington, 2006] is cited as an example of structured clinical communication, not as evidence about presentations.
- **L11** cites primacy and recency effects in list recall [Murdock, 1962] and the colonoscopy study of retrospective evaluations [Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996], again with the explicit caveat that neither concerned presentations. It also cites the information-gap account of curiosity [Loewenstein, 1994] and research on implementation intentions [Gollwitzer, 1999], which L03 already cites.
- **L12** cites the redundancy effect [Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller, 2004; Mayer, 2020], a psychological analysis of slide presentations [Kosslyn et al., 2012], studies of sentence headlines and assertion–evidence slides [Alley et al., 2006; Garner & Alley, 2013], graphical perception [Cleveland & McGill, 1984] and the prevalence of red–green colour vision deficiency [Birch, 2012]. The text states that the slide-design studies were conducted with students in educational settings, so the size of their effect should be treated with caution. The minimum font sizes are labelled as **professional guidance**.
- **No clinical advice.** All clinical content is fictional or composite and illustrates communication only. Numbers in the examples belong to fictional speakers and are not presented as facts about medicine.
- **References** follow the same rule as before: they must be checked against the originals by the Academic Director before launch (open item F8 in the register).

**Pronunciation.** These lessons contain no IPA; phrase-bank guidance marks only pauses (//) and the stressed word, as in L07.

**Data protection and integrity built into the content.** L11 and L12 teach that patient stories must be fictional or composite and that identifiable images require consent and institutional approval. The L12 assignment asks learners to describe slides in words rather than upload them, consistent with our decision not to collect learner files at this stage.

**Points I would particularly like your view on**

1. **Font-size guidance (L12).** I recommend body text of at least 24 points and headlines of at least 32 points. Some congresses publish their own minimums. Would you prefer a range, or a reference to the congress template?
2. **SBAR (L10).** Is SBAR familiar enough to your international audience to be a useful analogy, or should it be replaced or explained further?
3. **Call to action (L11).** The model closings include fairly direct requests (“from next Monday, please review…”). Is this register right across cultures, or should we show a softer alternative as well?
4. **The colonoscopy example (L11).** It is a well-known study in medicine, used here only as an analogy for why endings matter. Would you keep it, or prefer to rely on the list-recall evidence alone?
