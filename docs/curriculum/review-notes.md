## Part A — Author's self-assessment (honest)

**What works well**

- The lessons address real second-language speaking problems (cognitive load, rushing, apologising, fillers) rather than general motivation.
- Each lesson has a complete learning loop: objectives, teaching content, examples, common mistakes, an exercise, a server-scored quiz with explanations, an assignment and enforced completion criteria.
- Exercises and assignments ask the student to work on their own real talks, not on invented scenarios.
- The tone is respectful of experienced professionals and avoids exaggerated promises.

**Where the current drafts fall short of "educational excellence"**

1. **Depth.** The written teaching content is only 382–565 words per lesson. For a 50–60-minute lesson in a premium programme, this is too thin: it reads as a well-structured summary rather than a full lesson.
2. **Examples.** Only two examples per lesson, and they are short. Doctors learn most from detailed, realistic before-and-after examples across specialties and settings (congress, grand rounds, MDT, journal club, media).
3. **No language support.** For non-native speakers, the single most useful addition would be a **phrase bank** in every lesson: ready-to-use professional English phrases (for example for signposting, buying thinking time or presenting data), with notes on stress and register.
4. **Quizzes test recall more than application.** The 3–4 questions mostly check understanding. Scenario-based questions ("A chair interrupts your talk at minute 7 of 10. What do you do?") would assess judgement better.
5. **No model answers.** Exercises have no worked example or self-check criteria, so a student cannot tell what a good response looks like.
6. **Assignments lack assessment criteria.** Instructors (Stage 5) will need a short rubric per assignment.
7. **Two unsupported empirical claims** that must be reworded, because medical audiences will expect evidence:
   - L01: "A speaker with an accent and a clear structure is **consistently** easier to follow than a fluent speaker without one."
     → Proposed: "In practice, a speaker with an accent and a clear structure is usually easier to follow than a fluent speaker without one."
   - L01 example: "Audiences **consistently** rate Speaker B as more confident and more expert."
     → Proposed: "Most listeners would perceive Speaker B as more confident and more expert."
8. **Spelling decision needed.** The lessons use British spelling (recognise, behaviour, practise), but the product name on the website is "Professional **Program**" (American). British English would be "Programme".

**Factual statements used in the lessons** (presented as practical guidance, not research findings): increased cognitive load when speaking a second language; slow breathing with a longer exhale as a calming routine; around 130–160 words per minute as a comfortable pace for international audiences. None of these is presented as an Academy research result, and no statistics, endorsements or accreditation are claimed.

## Part B — Proposed content standard for all 36 lessons and workshops

I recommend approving this standard, upgrading these four lessons to it first as the reference model, and then writing the remaining 32 to the same standard.

**Core lessons (30) — each will contain:**

| Component | Standard |
|---|---|
| Learning objectives | 3–4, observable ("deliver…", "rewrite…", "answer…"), not "understand…" |
| Introduction | The professional situation and why it matters, 100–150 words |
| Teaching content | 3–5 sections, **1,200–1,800 words** in total; practical, specific to medical communication |
| Professional examples | At least 3 detailed before/after or annotated examples from varied settings (congress, grand rounds, MDT, journal club, teaching, media) and specialties. Composite and anonymised; no patient-identifiable details |
| Phrase bank | 8–12 professional English phrases for the lesson's situation, with notes on stress, register and common non-native errors |
| Demonstration brief | A short script for the instructor video (what is shown, what to notice) |
| Common mistakes | 4–6, each with the correction |
| Guided exercise | Step-by-step instructions, a model answer and self-check criteria |
| Knowledge check | 5–6 questions, at least half scenario-based; an explanation for every answer; pass mark 70% |
| Assignment | Applied to the student's own work, 150–400 words or (from Stage 4) a recording; 3–4 assessment criteria for instructors |
| Reflection | 2–3 questions linking to the student's goals from L03 |
| Completion criteria | Exercise saved, quiz passed, assignment submitted (enforced by the platform) |
| Duration | 50–65 minutes |

**Workshops (6):** 120 minutes (W6: 150), each with a briefing, 2–3 timed practice rounds, self- and peer-assessment checklists, debrief questions and a portfolio task. W6 is the live group coaching session with a human instructor.

**Language and integrity rules**

- British / international professional English throughout (subject to your decision on "Program").
- Short spoken-style sentences in examples; clear written style in the teaching text.
- No invented studies, statistics, testimonials, endorsements, accreditation or CME/CPD claims. Practical guidance is presented as guidance; any research reference will be real, verifiable and cited.
- No clinical advice. Medical examples illustrate communication only and are fictional or composite.
- Inclusive, internationally neutral examples across countries, specialties and career stages.

**Programme totals (unchanged):** 10 weeks · 12 modules · 30 lessons · 6 workshops · approximately 42 hours.
