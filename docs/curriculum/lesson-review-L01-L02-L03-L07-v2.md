### Introduction

Many doctors read, write and think in English every working day, yet feel noticeably less competent when they must speak it in front of an audience. A cardiologist who can discuss a complex case fluently with a colleague may lose a familiar word at the lectern, speed up, and retreat to reading the slides. This lesson explains why that happens, what international audiences actually need from you, and how to separate the accuracy of your English from the authority of your expertise. You will finish by identifying your own speaking triggers and replacing the most common self-undermining habit of second-language presenters — the apology — with a clear statement of purpose.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain how speaking in a second language increases mental workload, and why this affects fluency under pressure
- Distinguish accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility, and decide which of them matters most for your audiences
- Identify at least three personal speaking triggers and describe their effect on your delivery
- Replace an apologetic opening with a clear statement of context, purpose and route

### Teaching content

#### Why fluent clinicians lose words on stage

Speaking involves several processes running at once: deciding what you want to say, finding the words and grammar to say it, articulating it, and monitoring what you have just said. In your first language, finding the words and building sentences is largely automatic, so your attention is free for the audience and the argument.

In a second language, more of this work needs conscious attention. You search for the precise term, check the grammar of a sentence while you are already halfway through it, and listen to your own pronunciation. Under the pressure of a congress session or a grand round, the attention available to you shrinks further. The result is familiar to most second-language presenters: a word you know perfectly well disappears, sentences become longer and more tangled, and you start speaking faster to reach the end of a sentence before you lose it.

None of this is a sign of weak knowledge. It is a predictable consequence of divided attention. That is good news, because divided attention can be managed. Everything you prepare in advance — your structure, your opening, your transitions, the key terms and how you will pronounce them — no longer has to be produced live, and the attention it frees goes back to your audience.

> **Research evidence.** Models of speech production describe planning, formulation and articulation as separate stages that run in parallel. Research on second-language speech describes formulation in a second language as less automatic than in the first language, so it competes for limited attention. [1, 2]

#### Accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility

Three terms are often confused. Accent describes how different your pronunciation sounds from a particular variety of English. Comprehensibility describes how much effort a listener needs to understand you. Intelligibility describes how much of your message the listener actually understands. These are related, but they are not the same: a speaker with a strong accent can be fully intelligible, and a speaker with a mild accent can be hard to follow if the talk is rushed and poorly structured.

For your professional purposes, intelligibility and comprehensibility matter far more than accent. Your audience at an international meeting includes many listeners for whom English is also a second language. They are not comparing you with a newsreader; they are trying to follow your argument while processing technical content in a foreign language themselves.

There is, however, an honest caveat. Speech that is harder to process can be judged less favourably, even when the content is identical. The practical conclusion is not that you should try to hide your accent. It is that you should reduce your listeners' effort in every other way available to you: a clear structure, explicit signposting, a manageable pace, deliberate pauses and key terms that are stressed correctly and repeated. These are skills you can learn, and the rest of this programme is built around them.

> **Research evidence.** In a frequently cited study, listeners' ratings of accent strength did not reliably predict how much of second-language speech they understood: some heavily accented speech was fully intelligible. A separate experiment found that trivia statements were rated as less likely to be true when spoken with a non-native accent, which the authors attributed to greater processing difficulty. Work on English as an international language argues that a limited set of pronunciation features, including placing the main stress correctly, matters most for mutual intelligibility. [3, 4, 5]

#### Separating language accuracy from professional credibility

Credibility on stage rests on three things you already have or can prepare: your expertise, the quality of your preparation, and the clarity of your message. Language accuracy supports credibility, but it is not the same thing. A small slip with an article or a preposition rarely changes how an audience judges your competence. Treating every such slip as a threat increases self-monitoring, which in turn makes more slips likely.

A more useful approach is triage. Some elements of your talk must be accurate, because an error would change the meaning: numbers and units, negatives (“did” versus “did not”), drug names and doses, the names of outcomes, and your conclusions. Other elements can be imperfect without consequence: articles, most prepositions, minor tense errors and word order in a spontaneous answer. Prepare and rehearse the first group carefully; give yourself permission to let the second group go.

Numbers deserve particular attention. “Fifteen” and “fifty”, “thirteen” and “thirty” are easily confused, especially by listeners who are themselves non-native speakers. Show critical numbers on the slide, say them slowly, and where it matters, restate them in another form: “fifteen per cent — that is, roughly one patient in seven”.

> **Professional recommendation.** Before every talk, list the five pieces of information that must be understood exactly (numbers, doses, outcomes, conclusions) and rehearse those sentences aloud until they are automatic.

#### Know your triggers

Most second-language speakers are not equally anxious in every situation. Common triggers include being interrupted, questions from a senior colleague or a native speaker, a question asked quickly or with an unfamiliar accent, technical failure, being recorded, a strict chair with a timer, and the first minute of a talk. Each trigger tends to produce a recognisable pattern: a faster heartbeat and a dry mouth, but also changes in speech, such as rushing, losing words, over-explaining or falling silent.

Mapping your own triggers is the first practical step towards managing them. For each trigger, note what happens in your body, what happens to your speech, and what you would like to do instead. Then prepare a specific response. If an unclear question is a trigger, you can prepare a clarification phrase and practise it until it is automatic. Preparing the words in advance removes the need to formulate them under pressure, which is exactly when formulation is hardest.

#### Opening without apology

Many second-language speakers open with “Sorry for my English”. The intention is courteous, but the effect is the opposite of what you want: it directs the audience to listen for mistakes, and it frames your first impression around your language rather than your expertise.

Replace the apology with three short moves: context, purpose and route. Context tells the audience why the topic matters to them. Purpose tells them what you want them to take away. Route tells them how the talk is organised. If you are genuinely concerned about being understood, add a functional request rather than an apology: “Please stop me if anything is unclear.” This sounds confident, invites interaction and serves the audience.

### Professional examples

*Illustrative, composite examples; names and details are fictional.*

**Two openings, same content** — *International cardiology congress, 10-minute oral presentation*

Speaker A begins: “Good morning. Sorry for my English, I will try my best. So, this study is about delirium after cardiac surgery…” and reads a dense first slide quickly. Speaker B, whose accent is stronger, begins: “Good morning. Post-operative delirium affects many of our cardiac surgery patients, and it is often missed. [pause] Today I want to show you one change in our screening that helped us detect it earlier. [pause] I’ll describe what we changed, what we found, and what we are still unsure about.” Most listeners would perceive Speaker B as more confident and more expert, even though Speaker A’s English is more accurate. Speaker B has reduced the listeners’ effort and given them a reason to listen.

**Losing a word at grand rounds** — *Grand round, respiratory medicine, presented by a specialty trainee*

Midway through a case, the presenter cannot retrieve the word “pneumothorax”. Instead of stopping, she paraphrases: “The repeat X-ray showed air in the pleural space — the lung had partially collapsed — a pneumothorax.” The word returns as she paraphrases. The audience hears a natural explanation, not a failure. The strategy works because paraphrase uses words that are easier to retrieve under pressure, and it gives the memory a moment to recover the precise term.

**A fast question from a senior native speaker** — *Question-and-answer session after a research presentation*

A professor asks a long, rapid question with several clauses. The presenter catches only the end. Rather than guessing, she says: “Thank you. Could I ask you to repeat the first part of your question?” and then: “If I’ve understood you correctly, you’re asking whether the effect persisted beyond six months.” The professor confirms, and her answer is precise. Asking for clarification is standard practice among experienced speakers, including native speakers; it signals care, not weakness.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Apologising for your English at the start | State context, purpose and route; if needed, add “Please stop me if anything is unclear.” |
| Writing a word-for-word script and reading it under pressure | Script only the opening, transitions and critical sentences; speak the rest from key phrases. |
| Correcting small grammar slips aloud | Continue; correct only errors that change meaning, such as numbers or negatives. |
| Guessing the meaning of a question you did not fully hear | Ask for repetition and paraphrase the question before answering. |

### Introduction

Every experienced speaker feels some arousal before an important talk: a faster heartbeat, a dry mouth, restless hands. For many second-language speakers these sensations are stronger, and they arrive exactly when the extra effort of speaking in English is greatest. This lesson treats nervousness as something to manage rather than eliminate. You will learn what is happening in your body, how the way you interpret it changes your performance, and how to build a short, reliable routine for the day, the hour and the minute before you speak. You will also prepare the most difficult part of any talk — the first thirty seconds — and a recovery plan for the moment your mind goes blank.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Recognise the physical and verbal signs of performance arousal in yourself
- Apply a reappraisal statement and a slow-breathing routine before speaking
- Write and rehearse an opening of no more than 70 words with planned pauses
- Use a three-step recovery technique when you lose your place during a talk

### Teaching content

#### What your body is doing, and why it is not the enemy

Before a talk, your body prepares for a demanding task. Stress hormones raise your heart rate and breathing, redirect blood flow and sharpen attention to threat. The signs are familiar: palpitations, a dry mouth, cold hands, a tight throat and an urge to move. In speech, the same arousal often shows as a faster pace, a higher pitch, shallow breathing that leaves you short of air at the end of sentences, and a tendency to rush through the opening.

A common goal is to stop feeling nervous. It is also an unrealistic one. Arousal is partly automatic, and trying to suppress it usually increases self-monitoring — the very process that already competes for attention when you speak a second language. A more useful goal is regulation: bringing arousal into a range in which it sharpens rather than scatters your attention, and keeping its effects on your speech under control.

It helps to separate the sensation from the story you tell about it. The sensation is physical. The story — “I am going to fail” or “my body is getting ready to perform” — is an interpretation, and it is the interpretation you can change.

#### Reappraisal: changing the interpretation, not the feeling

Reappraisal means deliberately reinterpreting arousal as a resource. Instead of telling yourself to calm down, you name the sensation as preparation: “This is energy. My body is getting ready.” The advantage is that this statement is true, so you do not have to argue with your own heartbeat.

Use a short sentence that you can say silently in the minutes before you speak. Some speakers prefer “I am excited to share this”; others prefer a more neutral version such as “This energy will help me focus.” Choose wording you can say honestly. Rehearse it in low-stakes situations first, such as before a ward meeting or a journal club, so that it is automatic when the stakes are high.

> **Research evidence.** In laboratory studies, participants instructed to reinterpret pre-performance arousal as helpful showed more adaptive physiological responses, and participants who said “I am excited” before a public speaking task were rated as more persuasive, competent and confident than those who tried to calm down. [1, 2]

#### Slow breathing

Breathing is the one part of the stress response you can control directly. Slow breathing with an exhale that is longer than the inhale is widely used to reduce physiological arousal. A practical pattern is to inhale through the nose for four counts and exhale gently through the mouth for six counts, for about two minutes. Nobody in the room needs to notice; you can do it while waiting to be introduced.

Breathing also matters during the talk. Anxious speakers often take shallow breaths and then try to say a long sentence on what remains. Plan short sentences and natural breathing points in your opening, and breathe at every pause. A breath taken at a pause is invisible to the audience; a sentence that runs out of air is not.

> **Research evidence.** A systematic review of slow-breathing techniques (around six breaths per minute) reported associations with increased heart-rate variability, relaxation and reduced anxiety. The four-in, six-out pattern used here is a practical recommendation based on this approach rather than a tested protocol for presentations. [3]

#### A pre-speaking routine: the day, the hour, the minute

Routines work because they replace decisions with habits. On the day before, finish your slides, rehearse the full talk aloud once with a timer, and rehearse the opening and conclusion several times. Do not plan to make major changes on the day itself.

One hour before, check the room and the technology: the clicker, the laser pointer, the microphone, the video files and the timer the chair will use. Then stop editing. Last-minute changes create new material that has not been rehearsed, which increases load at the worst possible moment.

Ten minutes before, do two minutes of slow breathing and say your reappraisal statement. Drink water to prevent a dry mouth. One minute before, stand or sit with both feet on the floor, feel the contact, and silently rehearse your first sentence. When you reach the lectern, arrange your notes, look at the audience, and take one breath before you speak. The silence will feel long to you and normal to everyone else.

> **Professional recommendation.** Rehearse the routine itself, not only the talk. Use it before smaller occasions for two or three weeks so that it becomes automatic.

#### The first thirty seconds

Arousal usually peaks in the opening, and this is where second-language speakers most often rush. Prepare the first thirty seconds word for word: no more than 70 words, in short spoken sentences, with at least two marked pauses. Use the structure from Lesson 1: context, purpose and route. Rehearse it aloud until it feels natural, and then rehearse it standing up, because posture changes breathing and voice.

Open with a sentence that is easy to say. Avoid starting with long technical terms, numbers or complex grammar. “Good morning. Thank you, Chair” is followed by a pause, then by a short sentence that tells the audience why the topic matters. Once the opening is behind you, most speakers find their rhythm. You are not memorising the whole talk; you are building a reliable way into it.

#### When your mind goes blank

Almost every speaker loses their place at some point. What distinguishes experienced speakers is not that it never happens, but that they have a plan. Use three steps. First, pause and breathe; a silence of three or four seconds is not noticed as a problem. Second, look at your slide or your notes for the key phrase. Third, bridge back with a short summary of what you have just said: “So, to recap — the screening was feasible. Let me now turn to the results.”

Summarising buys time, helps the audience, and usually brings back the next point. Prepare one recap sentence for each section of your talk in advance, so that you never have to compose it under pressure.

#### A note on medication

Some clinicians ask about medication for performance anxiety. This is a clinical decision for the individual and their own doctor, and it is outside the scope of this programme. The techniques in this lesson are educational and can be used alongside any approach you choose.

### Introduction

Authority in medical speaking does not come from sounding like someone else. It comes from a clear sense of what you know, how you want to be heard, and what you are working to improve. This lesson turns the general wish to “be more confident” into a professional speaker identity and a concrete development plan. You will audit the strengths you already bring as a clinician or researcher, define the voice you want audiences to recognise, learn the language of measured scientific confidence, and set three specific, observable goals for the ten weeks of the programme — starting from an honest baseline recording of how you speak today.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Identify at least three strengths you already bring to professional speaking
- Define your professional voice in three words and link each word to a concrete delivery choice
- Use appropriate hedging and boosting language to sound confident without overstating evidence
- Write three specific, observable goals with if–then plans and a baseline for each

### Teaching content

#### What authority means for a medical speaker

Audiences at medical meetings judge speakers quickly, and they judge them on more than language. A useful framework, which goes back to classical rhetoric, distinguishes three sources of persuasion: the credibility of the speaker, the logic of the argument, and the connection with the audience. For a medical speaker, credibility comes from expertise and integrity: knowing the field, being precise about what the evidence shows, and being honest about its limits. Logic comes from a clear structure and a well-chosen core message. Connection comes from showing why the topic matters to the people in the room.

Notice that none of these depends on a native accent. A second-language speaker who is precise, well organised and relevant has every source of authority available. The work of this programme is to make those qualities audible: to deliver them clearly enough that the audience recognises them.

#### Strengths you already have

Medical professionals bring real advantages to the podium, and it is worth naming them explicitly. You explain complex information to patients and families, often under time pressure and in difficult circumstances. You present cases in a structured way, from history to plan. You are used to reasoning from evidence, weighing uncertainty and making decisions. Many of you teach students and junior colleagues. Some of you have experience of speaking in two or three languages, which gives you a sharp awareness of how meaning can be lost.

Begin with an audit. List the situations in which you already communicate well — a family meeting, a handover, a bedside teaching session — and ask what you do there that works. Often the answer is surprisingly transferable: you slow down for a worried family, you check understanding, you choose simple words for a complex idea. These are presentation skills. Part of your development is simply bringing them onto the stage.

#### Defining your professional voice

Choose three words that you would like an audience to use about you after a talk: for example “clear, practical, warm” or “precise, calm, curious”. Avoid words that describe a personality you do not have. A calm and precise speaker does not need to become theatrical to be effective; trying to perform a different personality adds yet another task to an already demanding situation.

Each word should guide concrete choices. “Clear” might mean one message per slide and a summary at the end of each section. “Practical” might mean ending every talk with what the audience can do on Monday. “Curious” might mean opening with a genuine question from your own practice. Writing down these links turns an abstract identity into decisions you can rehearse and evaluate.

> **Professional recommendation.** Write your three words on the first page of your speaker notes for every talk during the programme, and check one delivery choice against each word when you rehearse.

#### The language of measured confidence

Scientific English has a particular way of sounding confident. Overstating results (“this proves”) damages credibility with expert audiences, but excessive caution (“maybe it could perhaps be possible that”) sounds uncertain. Authority lies in matching the strength of your language to the strength of your evidence.

Use boosters when the evidence is strong: “clearly”, “consistently”, “the data show”. Use hedges when it is not: “our data suggest”, “this may reflect”, “in our population”. Distinguish your findings from your interpretation: “We found…; we think this is because…”. State limitations directly rather than apologetically: “The main limitation is the sample size.” Second-language speakers often either avoid hedging altogether or stack several hedges in one sentence. One accurate hedge, placed where the uncertainty actually lies, is both more confident and more honest.

#### Goals that can be observed and measured

Vague goals such as “be more confident” or “improve my English” are hard to act on and impossible to evaluate. Useful speaking goals describe behaviour that you or an observer can see or measure: speaking at a comfortable pace, pausing before each key result, opening without an apology, or answering three questions without looking at your notes.

Add an if–then plan to each goal: a specific situation and a specific action. “If the chair shows the two-minute sign, then I will move to my conclusion slide and say my take-home message.” Plans of this kind link the goal to the moment when you need it, which is precisely when you have least attention to spare.

> **Research evidence.** Research on goal setting has found that specific, challenging goals generally lead to better performance than vague instructions to “do your best”. Studies of implementation intentions — plans in the form “if situation X, then I will do Y” — have found that they help people act on their goals. [1, 2]

#### Establish a baseline, then practise deliberately

You cannot see progress without a starting point. Record yourself giving a two-minute summary of your current work, as you would at the start of a conference talk. Use your phone or a laptop; in a later stage of the programme, the Speaking Studio will measure aspects such as pace and filler words for you. Listen once for content and once for delivery, and note what you hear: pace, pauses, fillers, and moments where you search for words.

Improvement comes from focused practice on one element at a time, with feedback, rather than from simply repeating whole talks. Choose one goal per week, practise it in short sessions, and record again. Over ten weeks, a sequence of short, targeted recordings is far more valuable than a single rehearsal the night before a congress.

> **Research evidence.** Research on expert performance distinguishes deliberate practice — focused effort on specific aspects of performance, with feedback — from simple repetition, and associates it with the development of expertise. [3]

### Introduction

Under pressure, most presenters speed up. In a second language the effect is stronger: we hurry to reach the end of a sentence before we lose the words, and we fill every silence because silence feels like failure. The audience pays the price. Listeners need time to process technical information, and many of them are processing it in a second language too. This lesson gives you control over three features of delivery that strongly affect how easily you are understood: pace, pausing and emphasis. You will measure your own pace, learn where pauses help and where they hurt, and practise placing stress on the words that carry your meaning — including the numbers that international audiences most often mishear.

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Measure your speaking rate from a recording and compare it with a practical target range
- Divide spoken sentences into thought groups and place deliberate pauses before and after key messages
- Place sentence stress on the word that carries the main information, including contrastive stress
- Deliver numbers and data so that they are heard correctly by international audiences

### Teaching content

#### Pace: how fast is too fast?

There is no single correct speaking rate, but there is a practical range. Many presentation coaches recommend around 130 to 160 words per minute for international medical audiences, slower for dense data and faster for a story or a familiar point. Treat this as professional guidance, not a rule. A good pace is one at which your audience can follow your reasoning and still has a moment to think about what you have just shown them.

Measure your own pace rather than guessing. Record one minute of a talk, transcribe it or use an automatic transcript, and count the words. Many speakers are surprised: under pressure, rates above 180 words per minute are common, and they rarely feel fast to the speaker. In a later stage of the programme, the Speaking Studio will calculate this for you and show where in the talk you accelerate.

If you are short of time, cut content rather than speeding up. Speaking faster saves seconds but costs comprehension, and it usually increases errors and fillers. Decide in advance which slide you will skip if the chair shows you the two-minute sign.

> **Professional recommendation.** The 130–160 words-per-minute range is a widely used professional guideline, not a research-derived threshold. Use your own recordings, and your audience’s reactions, to find your best pace.

#### Thought groups and the deliberate pause

Fluent speakers do not pause randomly. They divide speech into thought groups — short units of meaning, often a phrase or a clause — and pause briefly between them. “In our cohort / of four hundred patients / readmissions fell / by a third.” Each group is easy to say on one breath and easy to process. Pausing in the middle of a unit, such as between an adjective and its noun or in the middle of a technical term, has the opposite effect.

Longer, deliberate pauses do specific work. A pause before a key result creates attention: the audience senses that something important is coming. A pause after it gives them time to absorb it. A pause when you show a new slide lets the audience read before you speak, so they are not choosing between your voice and the screen. These pauses feel very long to the speaker — two or three seconds can feel like ten — but they sound confident and controlled to listeners.

#### Fillers: what they are and what to do about them

Fillers such as “um”, “uh”, “so” and “you know” are often presented as errors. The reality is more nuanced. In ordinary conversation, fillers can signal that the speaker is planning the next words, and listeners use them. They are a normal feature of speech, including the speech of fluent native speakers.

In a formal presentation, however, frequent fillers can distract, and in a second language they often increase when you search for words. The aim is not to eliminate them completely, which tends to increase anxiety, but to reduce them by replacing many of them with silent pauses. A silent pause gives you the same planning time and sounds more authoritative. Start by noticing where your fillers cluster: very often they appear at the start of sentences and at slide transitions, which are exactly the moments you can prepare.

> **Research evidence.** Research on spontaneous speech suggests that “uh” and “um” are used to signal upcoming delays, and that listeners make use of them in comprehension. This is why the goal here is reduction and replacement with silent pauses, not elimination. [1, 2]

#### Stress: putting the weight where the meaning is

English uses stress to signal what matters. The stressed word in a phrase is slightly louder, longer and higher in pitch, and listeners use it to find the new or important information. “Mortality fell by a THIRD” tells the audience what to remember. “It was the DOSE, not the drug” uses contrastive stress to correct an assumption. Placing the stress on the wrong word, or on every word, makes speech harder to follow even when each individual sound is correct.

Word stress matters too, especially for medical terminology. Many medical terms look similar across languages but are stressed differently in English: car-di-o-my-OP-a-thy /ˌkɑː.di.əʊ.maɪˈɒp.ə.θi/, an-aes-THE-si-a /ˌæn.əsˈθiː.zi.ə/, ep-i-de-mi-OL-o-gy /ˌep.ɪ.diː.miˈɒl.ə.dʒi/. Misplaced word stress can make a familiar word hard to recognise, particularly for listeners who are themselves non-native speakers. Check the stress of your twenty most frequent technical terms in a good dictionary and practise them in full sentences, not in isolation.

> **Research evidence.** Studies of second-language speech have found that correctly placed sentence stress improved listeners’ recall and their evaluations of the speaker, and that misplaced word stress can reduce intelligibility, especially when the stress shifts to a later syllable. [3, 4]

#### Numbers and data

Numbers are where small delivery problems become real errors. “Thirteen” and “thirty”, “fifteen” and “fifty” differ mainly in stress and in the final sound: thir-TEEN and fif-TEEN are stressed on the second syllable when said on their own, THIR-ty and FIF-ty on the first. Said quickly in a large room, they are easily confused.

Protect your critical numbers in four ways. Show them on the slide. Say them slowly, with a pause before them. Restate them in another form: “fifteen per cent — roughly one patient in seven”. And when you present a graph, tell the audience where to look, pause while they look, and then state the conclusion. The sequence “look, pause, conclude” prevents the audience from reading one part of the slide while you describe another.

> **Professional recommendation.** Before each talk, underline every number in your notes and decide how you will say it and whether you will restate it.

#### Marking your script

The practical tool that brings these elements together is a marked script. Take the critical passages of your talk — the opening, the key result and the conclusion — and mark them by hand. Use a slash for a short pause between thought groups, a double slash for a deliberate pause, and capital letters for the one word in each phrase that carries the main stress. Then read the passage aloud twice: once naturally and once following your marks. Record both readings and listen to the difference.

You will not script the whole talk this way. The aim is to train your ear and your habits on the passages that matter most, so that pacing and emphasis become automatic in the rest of the talk.

### Professional examples

*Illustrative, composite examples; names and details are fictional.*

**Before and after: a results sentence** — *Internal medicine congress, results slide*

Before: “So um we found that the readmission rate was lower in the intervention group which was quite interesting and also the length of stay was shorter.” After: “In the intervention group, // readmissions fell / by a THIRD. // [pause] And patients went home / a DAY earlier.” The revised version has two thought groups per idea, a pause before and after the main result, and one stressed word in each phrase. It takes slightly longer to say, and it is remembered.

**Fifteen or fifty?** — *Multidisciplinary team meeting, online*

A registrar reports that a patient’s creatinine “went up by fifteen”. A colleague on a poor connection hears “fifty” and suggests urgent nephrology review. The discussion takes several minutes to untangle. Afterwards, the team agrees a simple habit for critical numbers: say the number, then restate it — “fifteen, one-five” — or show it in the shared document.

**Guiding the eye on a graph** — *Oncology grand round, Kaplan–Meier curve*

Instead of describing the curve while the audience is still reading the axes, the speaker says: “This is overall survival. Please look at the two lines after twelve months.” She pauses for three seconds. “They separate — and they stay separate.” She pauses again. “That is the main finding of this study.” The audience looks where she directs them, has time to see the effect, and hears the conclusion only once they are ready for it.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Speeding up when time is short | Cut a slide; keep your pace. |
| Filling every silence with “um” or “so” | Notice where fillers cluster and replace them with silent pauses there. |
| Stressing every word, so nothing stands out | Choose one stressed word per thought group. |
| Pausing in the middle of a technical term or phrase | Pause between thought groups, not inside them. |
| Describing a graph while the audience is still reading it | Direct the eye, pause, then conclude. |
| Saying critical numbers quickly | Show, slow down, and restate them in another form. |

### Practical exercise — Mark and measure a passage

Apply thought groups, pauses and stress to a real passage from one of your talks.

1. Choose a 60–100-word passage that contains a key result or a number.
2. Mark short pauses with / and deliberate pauses with //.
3. Capitalise the one word per thought group that carries the main stress.
4. Read it aloud twice — naturally, then following your marks — and time both readings.
5. Calculate your words per minute for each reading and note one difference you heard.

*Student prompt:* Paste your marked passage, your two timings or rates, and the difference you noticed. · *Minimum:* 250 characters

**Self-check**

- Pauses fall between thought groups, never inside a term or phrase.
- There is one capitalised word per thought group, not several.
- There is a deliberate pause before and after the key result.
- Numbers are protected (slowed, restated or both).
- You calculated both rates and described an audible difference.

**Model answer** *(fictional example; shown to the student only after they save their own response)*

> Marked passage (49 words): “We included / four HUNDRED patients / over sixty-FIVE. // In the intervention group, / readmissions fell / by a THIRD. // [pause] That’s roughly / one patient in THREE / who didn’t come BACK. // The effect / was LARGEST / in patients / living ALONE. // So, to summarise this slide, / earlier follow-up / helped MOST / where support at home / was WEAKEST.”
>
> Reading 1 (natural): 17 seconds → about 173 words per minute (49 ÷ 17 × 60). Reading 2 (marked): 22 seconds → about 134 words per minute.
> Difference: in the second reading, “by a third” sounded like the main point, and I no longer said “so” before every sentence.
